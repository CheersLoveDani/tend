# AuDHD Research Notes for Tend

## Summary

AuDHD (co-occurring autism and ADHD) puts two sets of needs in tension. The ADHD side needs **activation**: novelty, interest, and immediate reward. The autistic side needs **clarity and predictability**: unambiguous expectations, low sensory load, and no surprises. One practitioner source calls this a "double blocking mechanism". Without enough activation the person stalls, and when expectations are unclear the person freezes. A reminder app helps when it holds tasks outside the user's head, makes the first step tiny and concrete, pays out small, prompt, predictable rewards, and never punishes the user. It hurts when it nags, shames, overstimulates, or turns the user's tasks into demands.

**Evidence quality caveat:** Peer-reviewed research is thin for AuDHD specifically. The solid findings are Barkley's time-blindness and working-memory framing, Sonuga-Barke's delay-aversion work, the Kushlev et al. notification experiment, and the De Croon gamification review. Most of the AuDHD and app-design guidance below comes from clinicians and practitioners, or from vendor blogs. Treat it as informed hypothesis, and test it with real AuDHD users.

## 1. Executive dysfunction and task initiation

- Starting a task is the main everyday barrier. In AuDHD, ADHD's need for a dopamine "kick" combines with autism's difficulty starting when expectations are unclear. Others often misread this as laziness, and the person blames themselves. Recommended strategies are very small first steps ("open document"), short timed blocks with a clear end, if-then plans, and visually structured lists. ([Praxis Psychologie Berlin](https://www.praxis-psychologie-berlin.de/en/wikiblog-english/articles/executive-functions-in-adhd-and-autism-understanding-and-improving-cognitive-patterns-in-audhs))
- "Autistic inertia" (difficulty starting *or stopping*) pulls against ADHD's urge to jump to new ideas, so days swing between impulsive switching and freezing. Suggested strategies are tiny checkable steps, 5–25 minute blocks, and anchoring tasks to routines. ([Carmen, AuDHD tug-of-war](https://carmenauthenticallyadhd.substack.com/p/executive-dysfunction-tug-of-war); see also [AuDHD + Executive Dysfunction](https://carmenauthenticallyadhd.substack.com/p/audhd-executive-dysfunction))
- Reviewers of AuDHD apps list being frictionless, low cognitive load, and forgiving as selection criteria. These are opinions, not outcome data. ([Focus Bear](https://www.focusbear.io/blog-post/5-best-productivity-app-for-audhd-improve-lifestyle-and-enhance-productivity))

## 2. Time blindness

- Barkley coined "time blindness" and also calls it "temporal myopia". It is a difference in executive function, not a lack of effort. Recommended strategies are visible clocks and calendars, breaking tasks into stages so time estimates are realistic, short Pomodoro-style blocks ("start with two minutes"), and self-compassion. ([Understood.org](https://www.understood.org/en/articles/adhd-time-blindness))
- Barkley frames it as a deficit in *using* time to guide behaviour, not only in sensing it. ([Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/adhd-time-blindness.html))

## 3. Working memory: "out of sight, out of mind"

- The problem is not object permanence in the literal sense. It is trouble recalling and prioritising things when there is no visual cue. Recommended fixes are making things visible, labelled alarms, recurring tasks, and keeping the home screen focused on these tools. ([Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/object-permanence-and-adhd.html))
- Barkley recommends *externalizing* information to reduce working-memory load. He also warns that relying on tech for every memory task is "misguided for ADHD in many ways", because devices get lost, run out of battery, or fall out of sync. ([ADDitude](https://www.additudemag.com/working-memory-powers-executive-function/))

## 4. Dopamine, interest-based motivation, immediate reward

- Dodson's "interest-based nervous system": importance alone often fails to start a task. Interest, novelty, challenge, and urgency (PINCH: Passion/Play, Interest, Novelty, Competition, Hurry) do start it. **Novelty wears off**, so strategies need rotating. Motivation also varies from day to day. ([Neurodivergent Insights](https://neurodivergentinsights.com/adhd-motivation/)) This is a clinical model, and its dopamine explanation is a hypothesis.
- Research on delay aversion (Sonuga-Barke et al.) finds that people with ADHD prefer immediate rewards over larger delayed ones. Waiting itself is aversive. ([Southampton ePrints](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/68946/); [Exeter repository](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/rest/bitstreams/66675/retrieve))

## 5. Gamification: benefits and pitfalls

- **It can work.** A systematic review found that 19 of 27 experimental studies reported a significant positive effect of gamification on adherence, or a trend toward one. The authors note that adherence is measured inconsistently across studies. ([De Croon et al., KU Leuven](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/610140))
- **Streak loss and shame.** Practitioner and vendor writing describes this cycle: one missed day resets the streak to zero, the user feels shame, and they abandon the app. The writing recommends cumulative counts ("23 active days this month"), calling missed days "paused", and celebrating showing up. ([DEV: Why streaks make ADHD worse](https://dev.to/nucleusos/why-streaks-make-adhd-worse-and-what-actually-helps-5147); [DEV: no streaks](https://dev.to/nucleusos/adhd-support-app-no-streaks-why-gamification-hurts-more-than-it-helps-1ae0)) This is anecdotal, and some ADHD users *do* like streaks.
- **Overstimulation.** Heavily gamified apps (e.g., Habitica) suit ADHD users who like games. They fail users who are overstimulated by them. ([Alphonso Labs review](https://www.alphonsolabs.com/best-habit-tracker-apps-adhd-2026/amp/))
- **PDA and demand avoidance.** PDA is an anxiety-driven drive to avoid demands and keep control. Recommended approaches are declarative language ("The shoes are by the door", not "Put your shoes on"), real choices, collaboration, and cutting non-essential demands. ([ADHD Australia / PDA](https://www.adhd.com.au/autism/pathological-demand-avoidance-pda)) Too many reminders can feel like nagging. Choices only count if every option stays available. ([Outschool on PDA](https://outschool.com/neurodivergent/pathological-demand-avoidance-pda))

## 6. Autistic sensory needs and predictability

- Design guidance: respect the system reduce-motion setting and avoid animation the user can't control. Use soft, muted colours and avoid pure black and pure white. **Remove countdowns, timeouts, and urgency cues.** Use text labels on icons. Use literal language without idioms or sarcasm. ([Smart Interface Design Patterns](https://smart-interface-design-patterns.com/articles/design-autism/))
- Turn sound off by default, add an in-app motion toggle as well as the OS setting, keep navigation consistent, and let users customise. ([accessiBe](https://accessibe.com/blog/how-to-design-digital-environments-for-people-with-neuro-divergency); [VP0 low-stimulation UI](https://vp0.com/blogs/low-stimulation-ui-kit-for-autism))
- Intolerance of uncertainty is linked to anxiety in autistic adults, and research on it is ongoing. ([Aberystwyth University](https://www.aber.ac.uk/en/news/archive/2026/04/title-286920-en.html)) This argues for predictable reward rules over slot-machine variability.

## 7. Notification fatigue, and whether persistent prompts help or harm

- In a within-subject experiment (N=221, general population), participants reported significantly more inattention and hyperactivity in the week with notifications on than in the week with them off. Inattention in turn predicted lower productivity and wellbeing. ([Kushlev, Proulx & Dunn, CHI 2016](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Kushlev-CHI16.pdf)) The sample was not diagnosed with ADHD. The authors say the study does not show that phones cause ADHD.
- Repeated alarms tend to fade into background noise ("alarm fatigue"). This is anecdotal. ([AFFiNE blog](https://affine.pro/blog/setting-effective-reminders-adhd))
- **Synthesis:** a persistent prompt *helps* by externalising working memory (themes 3 and 1). It *harms* when it interrupts, escalates, or reads as a demand (themes 5 and 7). A prompt that is visible but silent, and that the user controls, gets the benefit without the cost.

## Design principles for Tend

1. **External memory that stays quiet.** The persistent Android notification is silent and low-priority (no sound, no vibration, no heads-up pop-up). It shows only a count plus the *next tiny step*, e.g. "2 things left · next: open the email". It never escalates by repeating, getting louder, or adding red badges. The user can turn it off, or limit it to set hours.
2. **The unlock screen is an invitation, not a demand.** Use declarative wording ("The laundry is still waiting, if you want it"). It always offers equal-weight choices: **Start a step**, **Later today**, **Not today**, and **Dismiss**. Set a daily cap on how often it appears (e.g. at most once every 2 hours, and a maximum per day), add quiet hours, and a one-tap "pause Tend for today".
3. **Partial credit is the core.** Every sub-step pays XP the moment it's checked. The first step is always tiny and concrete (the on-device LLM can suggest a split). Finishing 2 of 5 steps is a visible success and is never shown as "60% failed".
4. **No punitive streaks.** Don't use consecutive-day streaks that reset to zero. Show cumulative growth ("tended 14 days this month"). XP never decreases, levels are never lost, and the tree never wilts or dies. Missed days are just "rest days", which a garden needs anyway.
5. **"Not today" without guilt.** Snoozing or deferring costs nothing: no XP penalty, no "overdue" red, no counter of how often a task was snoozed. Overdue tasks move gently into "whenever you're ready" and are not stacked as debt. Offer an option to archive a stale task with a kind message.
6. **Predictable core, small optional surprises.** Base XP is deterministic and visible *before* starting, which gives autistic users certainty. A variable bonus is small, rare, and only ever *added* (e.g. "a new leaf appeared"). It is never needed to progress, and users can turn surprises off. Rotate cosmetic garden content (new plants, seasons) to address novelty decay without changing the rules.
7. **Rewards the user can edit, from a model that gives reasons.** The LLM suggests a value with a short reason ("3 steps, a bit tedious → 15 XP"). The user nudges it with ± buttons, and the app remembers their changes. There are no hidden calculations, and the model never judges or comments on the user's productivity.
8. **Sensory control.** Make the marimba and woodblock sounds soft and short. One master sound toggle (with separate volume) plus per-event toggles. Respect the system reduce-motion setting and add an in-app reduce-motion switch, which replaces growth animations with static crossfades. Use a muted natural palette with no pure black or white, no flashing or confetti storms, and haptics as an option.
9. **No urgency theatre.** No countdown timers, no "you're falling behind", no red overdue styling. Timed or due items show calm times in plain words ("this afternoon", "in about 2 hours") to help with time blindness, plus an optional visual time-remaining bar that the user starts themselves.
10. **Plain, kind, literal language and consistent layout.** No idioms or sarcasm, and text labels on icons. Buttons stay in the same place on every screen, and layout doesn't change between sessions. Gentle wording throughout: "paused", "resting", "whenever you're ready", never "failed", "broken", or "missed".

**Validate with users.** Run short trials with AuDHD testers and track whether people *come back after a gap*, rather than daily active use. That return is the real test of whether the app avoids shame.
